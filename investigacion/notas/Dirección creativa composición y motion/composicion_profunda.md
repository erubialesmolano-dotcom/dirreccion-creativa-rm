## Q1. What does perception research actually say about placement, balance, centre, thirds and golden ratio?

### Takeaway
The empirical record undermines the "rules" as stated: viewers prefer the CENTRE for single objects, prefer off-centre objects to FACE INTO the frame (inward bias), show no preference for thirds lines, and show no reliable golden-ratio preference. What does hold: context can justify unusual compositions ("representational fit"), and objects that face/move have directional "space" needs.

### Cited Findings
- [evidence] Palmer, Gardner & Wickens (2008) found a "pronounced center bias" for single-object placement. — [Palmer Lab](https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/spatial.html)
- [evidence] Left/right-facing objects are preferred off-centre, displaced toward the side OPPOSITE to where they face (i.e., they face into the larger space) = "inward bias". — [Palmer Lab](https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/spatial.html)
- [evidence] Langlois, Sammartino & Palmer (VSS 2012): right-facing objects preferred left of centre; motion direction overrode facing direction (backward divers); inward bias "decreased as implied speed increased." — [Palmer Lab](https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/spatial.html) / [VSS poster PDF](https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/pdf/Langlois.VSS.2012.Final.pdf)
- [evidence] Vertical biases are weaker but exist: eagles preferred higher, stingrays lower; objects supported from below get a "lower bias", from above an "upper bias" (Gardner, Nothelfer & Palmer 2008, hundreds of Corel stock photos). — [Palmer Lab](https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/spatial.html)
- [evidence] Rule of thirds test (Palmer, Hara & Griscom, VSS 2014; 3x3 forced choice with fish, dogs, eagles): thirds positions were NOT favoured and the centre was NOT avoided; centre was the most preferred position for every object and facing direction, strongest for symmetric forward-facing objects. Authors concede the rule may help artists explore off-centre placements that "work" in context. — [Palmer Lab](https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/spatial.html)
- [evidence] "Representational fit" (Sammartino, Gardner & Palmer 2010): people accept non-standard compositions "so long as there is a context that justifies" them. Gardner & Palmer: "greater aesthetic value often requires violating these expectations." — [Palmer Lab](https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/spatial.html)
- [evidence] Related objects (wine + cake) are placed close together; unrelated ones far apart (Leyssen et al. 2010) — proximity encodes relationship. — [Palmer Lab](https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/spatial.html)
- [evidence] Canonical size: people prefer small real objects to appear small in frame and large ones large (Linsen et al. 2010), though effect largely vanished when detail was equalised. — [Palmer Lab](https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/spatial.html)
- [evidence] Golden ratio: Friedenberg (2012, i-Perception 3(3)) — "There was no golden ratio effect" for triangles; preference tracked compactness/stability; triangles resting on a base and pointing upward rated highest, downward-pointing lowest; compact triangle chosen ~75% in paired comparisons. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485848)
- [evidence/practitioner] Goude & Hjortzberg (Stockholm), testing Arnheim's "structural skeleton" with a disk in a square: attention strives toward edges, clusters along the skeleton's axes, stabilises around the centre; "Dead center" is not dead. — [MIT balance talk](https://people.csail.mit.edu/fredo/ArtAndScienceOfDepiction/OtherTalks/balance.pdf)
- [practitioner] Visual weight factors (Arnheim tradition as summarised): position in the structural framework; size; intrinsic interest and isolation add weight; red heavier than blue; bright heavier than dark; simple geometric shapes heavier; top-heavier; empty space in front of a figure adds "balancing power." — [MIT balance talk](https://people.csail.mit.edu/fredo/ArtAndScienceOfDepiction/OtherTalks/balance.pdf)
- [practitioner] Arnheim's later book on the centre: *The Power of the Center*. — [UC Press](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/the-power-of-the-center/paper)
- [practitioner/critique] Hambidge's dynamic symmetry (root rectangles √2, √3, √5, φ) was critiqued in 1921 by Blake and Carpenter; Michael Quick argued complex versions could describe almost any shape; Malone (1979) called the theories discredited. — [Wikipedia: Jay Hambidge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jay_Hambidge)

### Inferences
- [inference] Practical translation for Claude: (1) Centre is a legitimate default for a single, symmetric, frontal subject (product hero, talking head, logo) — it is not "amateur"; it's the evidence-preferred position. (2) Off-centre is justified when the subject has a DIRECTION (gaze, motion, facing): put the space in front of it (lead room) — this is the inward bias, and it is the empirically strongest rule here. (3) Thirds and golden spirals are search heuristics, not perceptual laws. (4) For fast motion, lead room can shrink (Langlois: inward bias decreases with speed) — fast cars/runners can sit nearer centre.
- [inference] Short-siding (subject faces the near edge) violates inward bias deliberately → reads as wrong/confined, which is exactly why it works for unease (see Q3).
- [inference] Vertical placement should follow "where the thing naturally lives": flying things high, grounded things low; break it to make a thing feel displaced (a car floating high in frame = surreal ad).
- [inference] Golden ratio prompts in AI ("golden ratio composition") are mostly a style token; the generator will not compute φ. Better to describe positions concretely ("subject in left third, facing right, empty space to the right").

### Gaps
- Could not fetch McManus (2010, i-Perception "golden section" studies) or Green (1995) review; McManus is generally reported as finding at most a weak/inconsistent golden-section preference (unverified this pass).
- Locher's empirical balance studies (eye-tracking of balance, "pictorial balance" Gestalt) not fetched.
- Bertamini et al. 2011 (Liverpool PDF, on left/right bias in pictures) surfaced but not read; left-to-right/right-facing bias claims remain unverified here.

## Q2. Geometric systems: triangles, diagonals, dynamic symmetry, golden spiral, rule of odds

### Takeaway
Triangles and diagonals have real perceptual grounding (stability vs tension: upward-resting triangles read stable/attractive; diagonals read as motion/tension). Dynamic symmetry is a useful drafting grid with a weak historical/theoretical pedigree; use it as a tool for placing diagonals and "eyes," not as truth.

### Cited Findings
- [evidence] Base-resting, upward-pointing triangles are rated most attractive/stable; downward-pointing least — supports the classical "pyramid" composition for stability and the inverted triangle for instability. — [Friedenberg 2012, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485848)
- [practitioner] Molly Bang: diagonals suggest "motion and tension"; horizontals stability/calm; verticals energy/power that takes effort to maintain. — [SuperSummary, Picture This ch.2](https://www.supersummary.com/picture-this/chapter-2-summary)
- [practitioner] Dynamic symmetry is described as a grid "to promote continuity, flow, rhythm, and balance"; the "baroque diagonal" runs lower-left to upper-right, recommended as a starting point partly because Western viewers scan left-to-right; if the subject can't sit on it, place elements parallel to it; keep separation from the background (Tavis Leaf Glover's teaching). — [PictureCorrect](https://www.picturecorrect.com/?p=75360)
- [practitioner] Hambidge (1867–1924), *Dynamic Symmetry: The Greek Vase* (1920), *The Elements of Dynamic Symmetry* (1926); used by George Bellows, Maxfield Parrish and others; applied to photography incl. Cartier-Bresson. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jay_Hambidge)

### Inferences
- [inference] Triangular composition, operational checklist (practitioner standard, unverified this pass): (a) apex = most important element (face, product, logo); base = supporting mass (bodies, table, ground). (b) Three-figure staging: put the dominant character at apex (standing/closer/higher), two others at base corners — classic in Renaissance group painting and in ensemble shots (e.g., Godfather office scenes, Last Supper-derived tableaux). (c) "Triangle of gaze": A looks at B, B at C, C at A (or two characters both looking at a third object) — the eye circulates and stays inside the frame. (d) Inverted triangle (point down) = precariousness, use for villains looming, collapse moments.
- [inference] Dynamic symmetry construction for 16:9 (≈1.78, close to √3 = 1.732): draw both main diagonals; from each corner drop a perpendicular onto the opposite diagonal ("reciprocal"); intersections = "eyes" for focal points. Put the main subject on an eye, align limbs/props/shadows parallel to diagonals or reciprocals. For 9:16 the same construction rotates: diagonals become steep (~60° from horizontal) → strong vertical drive; reciprocals become near-horizontal bands useful for text placement.
- [inference] Rule of odds (practitioner, unverified): 3 or 5 items read as a group with a natural centre; 2 creates a duel/comparison; 4 tends to split into pairs. Use 2 deliberately for confrontation, 3 for product lineups (hero in the middle, bigger/closer).
- [inference] Golden spiral overlays (Fibonacci) are post-hoc rationalisations; the evidence (Q1) does not support φ preference. If used, use it as "curve leading eye into a focal point near a third" — functionally equivalent to a curved leading line.
- [inference] AI prompting: "triangular composition, three figures, tallest at apex in the centre, two seated at lower corners"; "strong diagonal composition, subject rising from bottom-left to top-right"; "dutch angle 20 degrees" for tension. Generators respond better to explicit positional/body language than to "dynamic symmetry".
- [inference] When to break: symmetric centred frames (Kubrick, Anderson) reject diagonals to feel static/controlled/uncanny; break triangles when you want the group to read as fractured (characters in a line or scattered).

### Gaps
- Dan Mumford's dynamic-symmetry video lessons and Andrew Loomis (*Creative Illustration*) not fetched; their specific constructs (Loomis "thumbnail" layouts, "radiating lines", "cross" etc.) remain unverified.

## Q3. Lines and vectors: leading lines, body lines, gaze, lead room, short side, headroom

### Takeaway
The strongest vectors in a frame are gaze and motion (they override facing, per Palmer), followed by body gesture and actual lines. Lead room is the empirically backed rule; short-siding is its meaningful violation.

### Cited Findings
- [evidence] Facing direction creates an inward-space preference; motion direction overrides it. — [Palmer Lab](https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/spatial.html)
- [practitioner] Short siding: face placed near the edge it looks toward, "crushing look-space and leaving surplus air behind the head"; "Deny the character air and the scene tightens without a closer lens." Example: *The Silence of the Lambs*, short-sided faces make conversations feel like "cages." — [Melies](https://melies.co/cinematic-techniques/composition/short-siding)
- [practitioner] Lines are often "generally only in our heads" — implied by juxtaposed elements; lines define shapes and direct the eye. — [Willamette course PDF](https://people.willamette.edu/~gorr/classes/cs145/resources/DesignComponents.pdf)
- [practitioner] Empty space in front of a figure adds "balancing power" (Arnheim tradition). — [MIT balance talk](https://people.csail.mit.edu/fredo/ArtAndScienceOfDepiction/OtherTalks/balance.pdf)
- [practitioner] Kurosawa: individual blocking is exaggerated (nervous characters pace, outraged stand tall) and actors repeat a signature gesture — body lines as character signal. — [EFAP Kurosawa transcript](https://amara.org/subtitles/SoHQMOziYeK5/en/1/download/Akira%20Kurosawa%20-%20Composing%20Movement.en.txt)

### Inferences
- [inference] Hierarchy of vectors (strongest → weakest), useful as a mental model: gaze of a face > pointing gesture/limb > motion direction > lines of action in body (spine curve, "C" and "S" curves from animation) > architectural leading lines > implied lines between aligned objects.
- [inference] Leading lines: roads, rails, corridors, shadows; they should terminate at or near the focal point; converging lines (one-point perspective, Kubrick corridors) = inevitability/control; lines exiting the frame lead the eye out (use to transition or to make the frame feel "open").
- [inference] Eyeline as composition: in OTS/reverse shots keep eyelines matched (A looks frame-right, B looks frame-left) and at consistent heights; higher eyeline of one character relative to the other = dominance.
- [inference] Headroom (unverified this pass): standard = eyes around the upper third line; tight headroom (cutting forehead) in close-ups = intimacy/intensity (Leone, Fincher); excessive headroom = smallness/isolation/loneliness. In 9:16, headroom must account for platform UI at top (status bar/username) and bottom (caption/CTA) — put eyes around 35–42% from top, not on the top third of the full frame.
- [inference] Lead room in 9:16: horizontal lead room barely exists (frame is narrow), so convert direction into VERTICAL vectors (looking up/down, rising motion) or use depth (looking into the lens axis). Profile shots in 9:16 often feel short-sided automatically — choose 3/4 views.
- [inference] AI prompting: "looking off-frame to the right, generous negative space on the right", "profile facing left edge, face pressed against left edge of frame (short-sided), large empty space behind head" (for unease), "head cropped at forehead, extreme close-up".

### Gaps
- Animation "line of action" sources (Preston Blair, Loomis gesture) not fetched.

## Q4. Visual weight, figure-ground, Gestalt, colour and light as composition

### Takeaway
Weight is a sum of size, brightness/contrast, saturation/warm colour, isolation, faces/text (high-interest content), and position. Composition is largely controlling contrast: Block's principle — contrast raises intensity, affinity lowers it.

### Cited Findings
- [practitioner] Bruce Block: contrast (difference) produces greater visual intensity; affinity (similarity) less. Basic components: space, line & shape, tone/value, colour, movement, rhythm (as taught). — [Willamette PDF](https://people.willamette.edu/~gorr/classes/cs145/resources/DesignComponents.pdf); book: [Routledge, The Visual Story 3rd ed.](https://www.routledge.com/The-Visual-Story/Block/p/book/9781138014152)
- [practitioner] Weight: red > blue, bright > dark, isolation and intrinsic interest add weight, simple shapes heavier. — [MIT balance talk](https://people.csail.mit.edu/fredo/ArtAndScienceOfDepiction/OtherTalks/balance.pdf)
- [practitioner] Molly Bang: light/white backgrounds feel safe, dark ones uneasy; bright colours "glow" against dark; viewers group shapes by colour more easily than by shape; contrast is what lets us distinguish anything. — [SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/picture-this/chapter-2-summary)
- [evidence] Proximity encodes relationship: related objects placed close (Leyssen et al.). — [Palmer Lab](https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/spatial.html)

### Inferences
- [inference] Gestalt applied to frames (standard principles, unverified this pass): Proximity — group characters by distance to show alliances (gap = rift). Similarity — same colour/costume groups people (team, army); one different = protagonist (accent). Closure — partial shapes in frame (cropped logo, silhouette) are completed by the viewer; good for teaser reveals. Continuity — the eye follows smooth paths; align elements across a cut. Common fate — elements moving together read as one unit (crowd, flock, kinetic type moving as a block). Prägnanz — the simplest reading wins; clutter destroys hierarchy.
- [inference] Figure-ground: separate subject by contrast of value (light on dark or vice versa), colour (warm on cool), focus (shallow DoF), texture (detailed on smooth), motion (moving on static). For AI shots, explicitly request "subject separated from background by rim light, background darker by two stops, shallow depth of field".
- [inference] Colour composition: accent colour isolation (Schindler's List red coat — practitioner-famous, unverified here), complementary contrast (teal/orange), single saturated element in desaturated frame = guaranteed focal point. In ads: make the product the only saturated/warm element.
- [inference] Light as composition: pools of light define where to look (Caravaggio/chiaroscuro, Gordon Willis in The Godfather — unverified); rim/back light separates figure from ground; brightest area of the frame is the default first fixation; dark vignetting pushes weight to centre.
- [inference] Faces and text are "super-weights": any face, and any legible text, steal attention regardless of size. In motion graphics, never place a face and a headline competing at equal size; decide hierarchy.

### Gaps
- Wilson & Chatterjee (2005) balance-assessment test and Locher et al. eye-tracking studies not verified.
- Saliency research (Itti & Koch) on colour/contrast pop-out not fetched.

## Q5. Shape language and emotional geometry (Bang, Kandinsky, Dondis)

### Takeaway
Molly Bang's ten principles give an operational emotion-to-geometry table; it is practitioner (picture-book) knowledge consistent with the Friedenberg stability finding.

### Cited Findings
- [practitioner] Bang's principles: (1) horizontals = stability/calm; (2) verticals = energy/power, effortful; (3) diagonals = motion/tension; (4) upper half = freedom, happiness, power, spirituality; lower half = grounded, heavier, constrained; (5) centre = strongest focal point, off-centre more dynamic and implies off-frame space; (6) white/light backgrounds = safety, dark = danger; (7) pointed shapes = fear, rounded = comfort/protection; (8) larger = stronger, smaller = vulnerable; (9) colour associations (red = blood/fire); colour groups more strongly than shape; (10) contrast enables seeing. — [SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/picture-this/chapter-2-summary)
- [practitioner] Bang developed these by asking how to make Little Red Riding Hood "more or less scary" with paper shapes. — [Virginia Tech ITMA](https://www.itma.vt.edu/courses/visual/lesson_3_2.html)
- [evidence] Upward-pointing base-resting triangles rated more attractive than downward; compactness preferred. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485848)

### Inferences
- [inference] Character/set shape language (animation standard, unverified): circles = friendly/soft (Baymax), squares = stable/stubborn/dependable (Carl in Up), triangles = dangerous/dynamic (villains, Maleficent). Apply to product ads: rounded UI/packaging = approachable; sharp angular graphics = aggressive/performance.
- [inference] Kandinsky (*Point and Line to Plane*, 1926, unverified this pass): point = rest; line = point in motion; horizontal = cold, flat; vertical = warm; diagonal = cold-warm; the "basic plane" has tensions — upper part lighter/freer, lower heavier; left = "towards home"/ease, right = toward adventure (Kandinsky's own claims; not empirical).
- [inference] Dondis (*A Primer of Visual Literacy*, 1973, unverified): basic elements dot, line, shape, direction, tone, colour, texture, scale, dimension, motion; perceptual "felt axis" and leveling/sharpening — a stimulus is either clearly balanced (leveling) or clearly unbalanced (sharpening); ambiguity (neither) is the error. This is a highly practical rule: commit to symmetry OR to clear asymmetry.
- [inference] Scale/dwarfing: tiny figure in vast landscape = insignificance/awe (Lawrence of Arabia, Interstellar, Dune — practitioner-famous); giant subject filling frame = power. Ads: product massive in landscape ("monumental product") = premium/iconic.

### Gaps
- Original Kandinsky and Dondis texts not fetched; their claims are summarised from memory.

## Q6. Depth, staging and blocking (Block, Welles, Spielberg, Kurosawa)

### Takeaway
Masters compose with movement and depth inside a single shot: Kurosawa layers five kinds of movement; Spielberg's "oner" stages coverage in depth so actors moving creates new compositions instead of cuts.

### Cited Findings
- [practitioner] Kurosawa's movements: nature (wind, rain, smoke in nearly every shot so even still characters have life), groups (crowds banding/splitting make emotion big), individuals (exaggerated blocking, signature gestures), camera (moves with clear beginning, middle, end), cutting on movement (and ending a scene on stillness then cutting into motion to unsettle). Example: *Seven Samurai*, Kikuchiyo's mood spreads through the village; a flag's motion carries the camera into the seven samurai with banner. Counter-example: *The Avengers* scene feels flat — one-directional camera, no clear start/end. — [EFAP Kurosawa](https://amara.org/subtitles/SoHQMOziYeK5/en/1/download/Akira%20Kurosawa%20-%20Composing%20Movement.en.txt)
- [practitioner] Spielberg oner: one unbroken shot that stays invisible; actors move, camera responds; blocking shifts focus between characters within a take; examples — *Raiders* (90-second take merging push-in, two singles, insert into one moving master), *Minority Report* (Steadicam moves only in response), *Jaws* (actor-driven, real ferry for background life), *Saving Private Ryan*. Core rule: "move your actors." Takes ~1–2 minutes, still shoots inserts for safety. — [EFAP Spielberg Oner](https://amara.org/subtitles/ml0fpo3zq2BN/en/1/download/The%20Spielberg%20Oner.en.srt)
- [practitioner] Depth cues/layers taught: vanishing points, gradual changes in colour, tone, focus, line strength; foreground/midground/background. — [Willamette PDF](https://people.willamette.edu/~gorr/classes/cs145/resources/DesignComponents.pdf)

### Inferences
- [inference] Block's space types (from *The Visual Story*, unverified this pass): deep space (depth cues maximised: convergence, size difference, overlap, atmospheric haze, textural gradient, movement toward/away from camera), flat space (planes parallel to the frame, telephoto, no convergence — Wes Anderson), limited space (flat but with overlap/size cues only), ambiguous space (viewer can't read scale/position — horror, disorientation). Pick ONE per sequence and use contrast between sequences for intensity.
- [inference] Welles (*Citizen Kane*, deep focus, Gregg Toland — unverified here): the boy Kane playing in the snow seen through the window in the background while adults sign away his future in foreground = depth staging carrying the story without cuts; low angles with ceilings visible = power/claustrophobia.
- [inference] Practical depth recipe for AI shots: "foreground: out-of-focus [object] framing left edge; midground: subject in sharp focus; background: hazy [location] with atmospheric perspective, smaller figures" — generators reliably render layered FG/MG/BG when explicitly listed.
- [inference] 9:16 depth: stack layers vertically (FG at bottom, subject in middle, BG at top) — vertical frames naturally suggest depth by height-in-plane cue.

## Q7. Frame grammar: open/closed, tight/loose, horizon, centre punch, quadrants, screen direction, 180°, Five C's

### Takeaway
These are mostly practitioner conventions; the perception-backed core is that directional subjects want inward space, the centre is strong, and reading direction may bias left-to-right scanning (claimed, weakly evidenced here).

### Cited Findings
- [practitioner] Centre "traps" the gaze; off-centre implies space beyond the frame (open-frame feeling). — [SuperSummary/Bang](https://www.supersummary.com/picture-this/chapter-2-summary)
- [practitioner] Left side emphasised, linked to reading direction (claim, with a dubious cerebral-dominance rationale). — [MIT balance talk](https://people.csail.mit.edu/fredo/ArtAndScienceOfDepiction/OtherTalks/balance.pdf)
- [evidence] Right-facing objects preferred left of centre (i.e., facing into space), a facing effect, not a universal left-right weighting. — [Palmer Lab](https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/spatial.html)

### Inferences
- [inference] Open vs closed frame (Braudy, unverified): closed = everything needed is inside, frame as a cage/stage (Anderson, Kubrick); open = action spills past edges, frame as a window (handheld docs, Malick). Tight vs loose: tight = pressure, detail, intimacy; loose = context, isolation, breath.
- [inference] Horizon height: low horizon (sky dominant) = freedom/aspiration/epic (Bang's "upper half = freedom"); high horizon (ground dominant) = weight, entrapment, labour; centred horizon = static/symmetry (only when intended). Tilted horizon = instability.
- [inference] Centre punch (Fury Road, per earlier pass): keep the focal point in the centre across rapid cuts so the eye never has to search — ideal for 9:16 fast-cut reels, since the centre is also the safest UI zone.
- [inference] Drive quadrant system: widely circulated practitioner claim that Refn/Sigel framed characters in specific quadrants to show isolation and relationships — I could NOT verify it in this pass (search returned nothing relevant). Treat as unverified.
- [inference] Screen direction: consistent left→right movement often read as "progress/going forward", right→left as "returning/resistance" (practitioner lore; LOTR's Fellowship mostly moving right-to-left toward Mordor is a commonly cited example — unverified). 180° rule: keep the camera on one side of the action axis so screen directions stay consistent; break deliberately (Kubrick's The Shining bathroom scene, Ozu's 360° space — unverified) to signal disorientation, shift in power, or a character crossing a moral line; cross legitimately via a neutral axis shot, a camera move across the line, or a cutaway.
- [inference] Mascelli, *The Five C's of Cinematography* (1965, unverified): Camera angles, Continuity, Cutting, Close-ups, Composition. Composition chapter emphasises simplicity, a single centre of interest, balance, and that movement and lines lead the eye.
- [inference] Short side vs long side in dialogue: standard OTS = character on the "long side" (looking across more space toward the other); short-siding both actors makes them feel opposed/trapped (*Mr. Robot* corner framing is a commonly cited modern example — unverified).

- Primary sources on Drive quadrants, Mr. Robot framing (Tod Campbell interviews), and screen-direction psychology studies not obtained.

## Q8. Grids for motion graphics and posters (Müller-Brockmann) and 16:9 vs 9:16 application

### Takeaway
Use a modular grid to make motion graphics feel designed; combine it with film rules (centre for fast cuts, inward space for directional elements) and platform safe zones.

### Cited Findings
- [practitioner] Proximity signals relationship (perceptually) — basis for grouping headline, sub and CTA tightly. — [Palmer Lab](https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/spatial.html)

### Inferences
- [inference] Müller-Brockmann (*Grid Systems in Graphic Design*, 1981, unverified this pass): modular grid of columns + rows + gutters; all elements align to module edges; consistent baseline; objectivity and order. For Remotion: define a 12-column grid for 1920x1080 (e.g., margins 96px, gutter 24px) and a 6-column grid for 1080x1920 (margins ~64px; top ~220px and bottom ~380px reserved for UI); snap text blocks and product to module edges; animate elements along grid lines (movement on grid = feels designed).
- [inference] Swiss poster principles transferable to motion: asymmetric layout with strong typographic hierarchy, flush-left ragged-right text, big scale contrast between headline and body (Block: contrast = intensity), one dominant image.
- [inference] 16:9 vs 9:16 per concept: thirds/lead room/OTS work natively in 16:9; in 9:16 prefer centre punch, vertical stacking (title top-middle, subject middle, CTA lower-middle above UI), vertical diagonals, low/high angles that exploit height, and single-subject frames. Two-shots in 9:16 → stack in depth (one FG one BG) rather than side by side.

### Gaps
- Müller-Brockmann and TikTok/Reels official safe-zone specs not fetched in this pass.

## Q9. Composition through time and composing for the cut

### Takeaway
A shot is a sequence of compositions: camera moves and blocking should move the viewer from one designed frame to another, each with clear start/middle/end; across cuts, keep eye position continuous (or deliberately jump) and use graphic matches.

### Cited Findings
- [practitioner] Kurosawa camera moves have a clear beginning, middle, end; cut on movement to hide edits; end scenes on stillness then cut into motion. — [EFAP Kurosawa](https://amara.org/subtitles/SoHQMOziYeK5/en/1/download/Akira%20Kurosawa%20-%20Composing%20Movement.en.txt)
- [practitioner] Spielberg links wide, OTS, single and insert compositions into one moving master; blocking changes the composition rather than cutting. — [EFAP Spielberg](https://amara.org/subtitles/ml0fpo3zq2BN/en/1/download/The%20Spielberg%20Oner.en.srt)
- [evidence] Inward bias reduces with implied speed — moving subjects tolerate less lead room. — [Palmer Lab](https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/spatial.html)

### Inferences
- [inference] Reveal techniques: pan/tilt/dolly to reveal a second subject (camera reveal), foreground wipe (object passes lens), rack focus (FG→BG shifts figure-ground), crane up (scale shift — tiny figure in vast space at end), push-in (tightening = rising importance/pressure). Design keyframes: first frame and last frame should each be a composed still.
