## 1. Theory: Chion, Murch, Thom, Lievsay (how sound adds meaning)

### Takeaway
Sound never just "accompanies" picture: it changes what the image means (Chion's added value), welds itself to visible events (synchresis), sets the sense of time, and works best where the image withholds information (Thom's "starving the eye"). Murch gives the practical ceiling: about 2.5 layers of the same type of sound, up to ~5 if spread across speech/FX/music.

### Cited Findings
**Chion, Audio-Vision**
- Added value: sound changes how the image is perceived and the image changes how sound is heard; Chion says "added value works reciprocally" (p. 21). — [Chion, Audio-Vision PDF](https://span2910.commons.gc.cuny.edu/wp-content/blogs.dir/8374/files/2019/08/Chion_Michel_Audio-Vision-edited.pdf)
- Synchresis = synchronism + synthesis: "the spontaneous and irresistible weld" between a sound and a visual event happening together (p. 63). — [Chion PDF](https://span2910.commons.gc.cuny.edu/wp-content/blogs.dir/8374/files/2019/08/Chion_Michel_Audio-Vision-edited.pdf)
- Synchresis is why falls, blows and explosions get their physical weight from sound; the same sound can be a crushed watermelon in comedy or a head blown apart in a war film, since verisimilitude comes "not from truth but from convention." — [Chion, Projections of Sound on Image](https://dalena.github.io/art5001/supplements/MICHEL_CHION_PROJECTIONS_OF_SOUND_ON_IMAGE.pdf)
- Three listening levels: causal (what made the sound), semantic (what it means, e.g. speech/code), reduced (the sound's own qualities). — [Chion PDF](https://span2910.commons.gc.cuny.edu/wp-content/blogs.dir/8374/files/2019/08/Chion_Michel_Audio-Vision-edited.pdf)
- Temporalization: "Sound temporalizes images in three ways" (p. 13) — it makes time in a shot feel more exact, immediate or vague; sounds are "vectorized" (have an irreversible beginning→end), while many images (a still photo) do not. — [Chion PDF](https://span2910.commons.gc.cuny.edu/wp-content/blogs.dir/8374/files/2019/08/Chion_Michel_Audio-Vision-edited.pdf)
- Punctuation: sound marks and emphasizes moments "without putting a strain on the acting or the editing" (p. 49); "rapid auditory punctuation" (whistles, bangs, pings) marks fast visual movements so viewers can follow them. — [Chion PDF](https://span2910.commons.gc.cuny.edu/wp-content/blogs.dir/8374/files/2019/08/Chion_Michel_Audio-Vision-edited.pdf); [Chion, Projections](https://dalena.github.io/art5001/supplements/MICHEL_CHION_PROJECTIONS_OF_SOUND_ON_IMAGE.pdf)
- Spatial magnetization: the image pulls a sound toward itself so it seems to come from the screen (p. 44). — [Chion PDF](https://span2910.commons.gc.cuny.edu/wp-content/blogs.dir/8374/files/2019/08/Chion_Michel_Audio-Vision-edited.pdf)
- Empathetic music takes on the scene's rhythm, tone and phrasing ("in harmony with the tone of the scene"). Anempathetic music shows "conspicuous indifference to the situation" and keeps its course regardless, which intensifies emotion against a cosmic backdrop. Examples: player pianos, music boxes, merry-go-rounds, dance bands; end of Carmen (crowd cheers while Don José stabs Carmen); noise can be anempathetic too (the shower still running after the Psycho murder). — [Chion, Projections](https://dalena.github.io/art5001/supplements/MICHEL_CHION_PROJECTIONS_OF_SOUND_ON_IMAGE.pdf)
- Added value by text: words structure how we see an image; Chion's example is an anchor saying "here are three small airplanes" — the statement seems to come from the image itself ("a ventriloquist's speech"). Music is "not 'redundant' in any way"; it can also act as a neutral signpost ("didactic counterpoint"). — [Chion, Projections](https://dalena.github.io/art5001/supplements/MICHEL_CHION_PROJECTIONS_OF_SOUND_ON_IMAGE.pdf)

**Walter Murch, "Dense Clarity – Clear Density"**
- Encoded (violet, speech: meaning via a learned code) ↔ embodied (red, music: meaning carried directly by the sound). Most FX sit in the middle ("yellow" sound-centaurs). Color describes how a sound works, not its emotional temperature. — [Murch, Dense Clarity PDF](https://rompus-public.pages.dev/portfolio/dense_clarity_by_walter_murch.pdf)
- Law of Two-and-a-Half: the mind tracks about 2-3 of something individually; beyond that the group becomes one entity. THX 1138 robot footsteps: 1-2 sets must sync; at 3+ sync barely matters, only overall pace/texture. Two dialogue streams can be followed, not three. — [Murch PDF](https://rompus-public.pages.dev/portfolio/dense_clarity_by_walter_murch.pdf)
- Up to 5 layers are tolerable if spread across the spectrum (dialogue, music, linguistic FX, musical FX, centaur FX); within one color the limit is ~2.5. The Apocalypse Now helicopter scene collapsed into noise at 6 layers; Murch cut it to 5. — [Murch PDF](https://rompus-public.pages.dev/portfolio/dense_clarity_by_walter_murch.pdf)
- Practical rules: mix in priority order (dialogue first); to add something, remove something (music was dropped at the "I'm not going" moment so dialogue and chaos could be heard); vary density over time like loudness; use depth (worldizing: re-recording sounds in real spaces) so foreground stays clear against softer backgrounds; prefer a gradual "slope to silence" over a hard cut, unless the cut coincides with a location change. — [Murch PDF](https://rompus-public.pages.dev/portfolio/dense_clarity_by_walter_murch.pdf)

**Randy Thom, "Designing a Movie for Sound"**
- Sound has value "when it is part of a continuum" — when it changes over time and resonates with other elements; loudness/fidelity alone don't make good design. — [Thom, filmsound.org](https://filmsound.org/articles/designing_for_sound.htm)
- Locations can be characters ("the place can therefore become a character"); sounds can transform across a story (a metal shear's rhythm morphing into a train clack). — [Thom](https://filmsound.org/articles/designing_for_sound.htm)
- POV is the most important cinematic element; most great sound sequences are POV sequences. "Characters need to have the opportunity to listen." — [Thom](https://filmsound.org/articles/designing_for_sound.htm)
- Sound works best with ambiguity, darkness, offscreen space, unfamiliar framing, slow motion, B&W, extreme close-ups/long shots; wall-to-wall dialogue masks sound. "Starving the eye will inevitably bring the ear" into play. Editors should hold shots after action ends to give sound room. — [Thom](https://filmsound.org/articles/designing_for_sound.htm)
- Example: a hidden character's rolling bottle reveals his presence before we see him; a single close-up of a pipe plus its sound establishes an apartment's ugliness for the rest of the film. — [Thom](https://filmsound.org/articles/designing_for_sound.htm)

**Skip Lievsay / Coens (No Country for Old Men)**
- Chigurh's introduction opens on an "eerie quiet," then a door slam and boot taps build anticipation; the Eagle Pass hotel scene builds tension from creaking floorboards, a tracking-device beep, a door handle popping off. Sound team: Skip Lievsay, Craig Berkey, Greg Orloff, Peter Kurland. The writer argues "you could ruin it with too much of a score." — [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/no-country-sound)

### Inferences
- For archive-photo videos (static images), Chion's vectorization point is central: the still image has no time; sound (ambience, a ticking clock, a breath) is what gives the photo "duration" and makes it feel alive. Each photo needs at least one sound layer that implies time passing.
- Synchresis means any sound synced to a cut, zoom or text pop will be read as "the sound of that thing" — so the choice of sound defines the meaning (a camera shutter on a photo = memory/document; a heartbeat = intimacy/threat; a typewriter = record/fact). Current skill's "pop on each word" is pure punctuation; overused it flattens hierarchy (Murch's 2.5 rule: per-word pops become one undifferentiated texture).
- Decision rule from Murch: in a 30-60s reel, the voice is "violet"; never stack two violet things (VO + lyrics). If the music has vocals, either duck to near-silence under VO, use an instrumental stem, or put lyrics only where VO is absent.
- Thom's "starving the eye" maps to AI-video: dark frames, blur, extreme close-ups, slow-mo and black frames are where sound should carry the story.

### Gaps
- Chion's "rendering vs reproduction" and the "acousmêtre" were not in the fetched excerpts (known from Audio-Vision ch. 5 and The Voice in Cinema; UNVERIFIED here). Rendering = sounds chosen to convey the sensation (weight, speed, pain) rather than reproduce the real sound.
- Murch's "worldizing" definition only from Dense Clarity summary; Ben Burtt, Gary Rydstrom and Sonnenschein were not fetched (Sonnenschein's book is a standard reference: Michael Wiese Productions, 2001).
- Gravity's use of vacuum silence not verified this session.

## 2. Silence and the sound of absence

### Takeaway
Silence is designed, not empty: it works by contrast with what came before, is usually a low constructed bed (room tone, body sounds), and total digital silence is reserved for a few shock moments. Fade toward silence gradually; drop dry only on a hard scene/location change or a reveal.

### Cited Findings
- A Quiet Place: the deaf daughter's POV is a "low hum" modeled on an anechoic chamber (blood, heartbeat); true total silence occurs only when she turns off her implant, the film's only moments of complete silence — treated as shocking because real life never offers zero sound. — [MPA / motionpictures.org](https://www.motionpictures.org/2018/04/the-a-quiet-place-sound-design-that-makes-audiences-afraid-of-their-own-noise/)
- Designers say deciding "what not to play" matters more than content; on wide shots footsteps are removed entirely, delicate sounds reserved for close-ups; with a very low baseline, small noises feel huge; "we're really hearing them more than we're seeing them." Goal: audience holds its breath until credits. — [motionpictures.org](https://www.motionpictures.org/2018/04/the-a-quiet-place-sound-design-that-makes-audiences-afraid-of-their-own-noise/)
- Murch: silence works best gradual and tension-creating, a "slope to silence," except when the cut accompanies a location change. — [Murch PDF](https://rompus-public.pages.dev/portfolio/dense_clarity_by_walter_murch.pdf)
- Honda Cog: most of the 120s spot has no music, only the sounds of parts colliding, until the car's CD player starts "Rapper's Delight"; VO by Garrison Keillor: "Isn't it nice when things just work?" — [Wikipedia, Cog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cog_(advertisement))
- No Country: "eerie quiet" then small, precise sounds (boots, beeps, floorboards) carry tension. — [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/no-country-sound)

### Inferences
- Decision rules:
  - Want a reveal to land → cut music/ambience 0.5–1.5 s before the reveal (or slope it down over 1–2 s), leave only room tone or one small sound (breath, click), then hit the reveal. The silence is the riser.
  - Want intimacy/memory in an archive photo → strip the music, keep a near-silent bed (room tone, distant city, clock), let a single detail sound (a page turning) do the work.
  - Never use pure digital zero mid-piece unless it's the shock moment; it reads as a playback glitch on phones. Keep a room-tone bed at very low level (practitioner rule, UNVERIFIED as a number; typical room tone sits roughly −50 to −60 dBFS).
  - Silence only works by contrast: it needs a denser section before it (Murch: vary density over time).
- Cog shows "sound-design-as-score": diegetic product sounds as the music, with the single music cue reserved as the payoff.

### Gaps
- Gravity (vacuum silence, sound through contact/vibration) and Rydstrom's statements on silence not fetched.
- No empirical source found on how long a silence can last in a short-form feed before scroll-away.

## 3. Music: choosing, editing, leading vs following

### Takeaway
Pick music for what the story needs (era, character, drive, emphasis), not just "nice"; iconic songs bring baggage. Edit music like a structure: count beats, mark 4-bar groups, cut on phrase boundaries, hide edits under hits, and use stems to drop vocals under VO. Counterpoint (anempathetic) music is a deliberate tool for irony/tragedy.

### Cited Findings
- Randall Poster: "The story is the guide"; music can set era, move the story, add emphasis or clarify a moment; "not really a science, more of an art"; tests unexpected choices ("compulsion to defy your own instinct"); iconic songs "carry a lot of emotional baggage" and can pull viewers out; music should be "part of the fabric of a film"; role includes setting "a common vocabulary for the music." He makes each film work "both creatively and financially." — [WIPO Magazine](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/wipo-magazine/articles/music-and-the-movies-an-interview-with-randall-poster-39133)
- Trailer-music editing: count tempo (4/4), marker on every beat, color-code the first beat of each four-bar group to see where to cut when extending/shortening; punctuate with hit (on a beat), suckback (into a hit at a key decision), riser (to a reveal), whoosh (between sections), drone (under a section for mood); shift mood by adding reverb at the tonal turn and booms under the track; swap stems (drop vocals, keep instrumental); blend a vocal stem with an instrumental of the same BPM aligned to beat markers. — [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/how-to-edit-trailer-music)
- Thom: music is most powerful integrated into the whole film; a well-produced score has little value if it sits apart from story. Music, dialogue and FX can each suggest mood, set pace, define character, place/time, smooth transitions, exaggerate action. — [Thom](https://filmsound.org/articles/designing_for_sound.htm)
- Chion: anempathetic music/noise (indifferent to the scene) intensifies emotion; empathetic music mirrors it. — [Chion, Projections](https://dalena.github.io/art5001/supplements/MICHEL_CHION_PROJECTIONS_OF_SOUND_ON_IMAGE.pdf)
- Murch: drop music when dialogue/chaos must be heard (trade elements). — [Murch PDF](https://rompus-public.pages.dev/portfolio/dense_clarity_by_walter_murch.pdf)

### Inferences (decision rules; practitioner knowledge, not each separately sourced)
- Choosing by feeling: tension/unease → drones, sustained notes, unresolved harmony, low register, no clear beat; nostalgia/memory (archive) → sparse piano/strings, tape warmth, slower tempo (~60-80 BPM), major-with-minor-color; drive/ambition (ads, founder stories) → steady pulse 100-128 BPM, building arrangement; irony/tragedy → anempathetic cheerful music under dark content. (UNVERIFIED as numeric rules; consistent with music-emotion research on tempo/mode, not fetched here.)
- Lead vs follow: music leads (cut to the music) in montages, intros, hype ads and openers; picture/VO leads (music follows and ducks) in narrative/testimonial sections. In story videos: let VO lead, music enters on emotional turns, not wall-to-wall.
- Cutting to music: cut picture on downbeats of bar 1 of a phrase for "big" moments, on off-beats for restless energy; place the twist/reveal on the drop or on the first downbeat after a silence; edit music at phrase boundaries (every 4/8 bars) and hide splices under a hit/whoosh or a transient; end the track on its real ending (use the song's final bar/cadence moved to the end, not a fade) — "backtiming" the ending.
- Mickey-mousing (music imitating every action) reads as comedy/cartoon; avoid in serious pieces, use deliberately for humor. Current "pop on every word" is a sound-FX form of mickey-mousing.
- Temp-love trap: once a team lives with a temp track, everything else sounds wrong; mitigate by testing 3 contrasting tracks against the same cut before committing.
- Licensed song vs score vs sound-design-as-score: song = era/identity/baggage (Poster); score = controllable emotion and length; sound-design-as-score = Cog/No Country style, most "premium" and least generic in ads.

### Gaps
- No primary interview with advertising music supervisors fetched; no source fetched for music-emotion research (tempo/mode), mickey-mousing or temp-love (both standard in film-music literature).

## 4. Sound in advertising and sonic branding

### Takeaway
The strongest ad soundtracks either use the product's own sounds as music (Cog) or a very short, distinctive, non-generic sonic logo (Netflix tudum, ~3-4 s) tested by what it evokes, not by liking.

### Cited Findings
- Honda Cog (Wieden+Kennedy, dir. Antoine Bardou-Jacquet, 2003): 120 s from two 60 s takes stitched; ~100 takes (the "606 takes" rumor debunked); £1M production, £6M campaign; almost no music, only part collisions until "Rapper's Delight" from the car's CD player; most-awarded commercial of 2004; highest BTAA jury score ever recorded. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cog_(advertisement))
- Netflix tudum: core by sound designer Lon Bender (two knocks of his wedding ring on a cabinet, a slowed anvil, muted hits) + "blossom" from a reversed guitar phrase by Charlie Campagna; brief: "had to be really short" (~3 s; result ~4 s), "more cinematic than electronic," tension-release, no spoken name, fit every genre; 20-30 options tested incl. a goat; blind word-association test (winner evoked "movie," "great"); ~1 year process; Hans Zimmer made the long theatrical version; "visual things... go out of style a lot more often than sounds." — [Twenty Thousand Hertz](https://www.20k.org/episodes/netflix)

### Inferences
- Lessons for Erik's brand closers: build a 1.5-4 s sonic signature from real recorded objects related to the brand (not a stock "logo whoosh"), avoid recognizable instruments (Netflix lesson), use it identically every time; test by asking "what does it make you think of?"
- Product hero shots: close-mic foley (cap click, pour, fabric, ASMR proximity) at high level with music ducked or removed is the ad equivalent of Thom's "let characters listen."

### Gaps
- Guinness "noitulovE", Apple's ad minimalism, Nike, McDonald's "I'm lovin' it", Intel bong: not fetched this session (budget). Guinness noitulovE (AMV BBDO, 2005) used Sammy Davis Jr.'s "Rhythm of Life" — UNVERIFIED here.

## 5. Sonic layering, mix, transitions; voice-over

### Takeaway
Build the mix by priority (voice first), respect the ~2.5-same-type-layers ceiling, spread layers across speech/FX/music, and vary density. Transitions: let sound cross cuts (J/L cuts, pre-laps) to bind shots and pull the viewer forward.

### Cited Findings
- Priority-order mixing, spectrum spread, trade elements, vary density, depth via worldizing — [Murch PDF](https://rompus-public.pages.dev/portfolio/dense_clarity_by_walter_murch.pdf)
- Wall-to-wall dialogue masks sound; editing for pauses gives sound room — [Thom](https://filmsound.org/articles/designing_for_sound.htm)
- Chion's "ventriloquist" effect: narration tells the viewer how to read the image — [Chion, Projections](https://dalena.github.io/art5001/supplements/MICHEL_CHION_PROJECTIONS_OF_SOUND_ON_IMAGE.pdf)

### Inferences (practitioner standards, UNVERIFIED numbers)
- Layer stack for a 30-90 s story video: (1) VO; (2) music; (3) one ambience/room tone per scene; (4) 1-2 spot FX per beat; (5) designed transitions. Never more than 2 FX events competing at the same instant.
- Ducking: music −8 to −12 dB under VO (sidechain with ~100-300 ms attack/release so it breathes), or carve 1-4 kHz on the music with dynamic EQ to keep voice intelligible; high-pass ambience/music below ~80-100 Hz on phone-targeted mixes.
- J-cut (next shot's sound starts before its picture) = anticipation/pull; L-cut (sound continues over next picture) = reflection/lingering; pre-lap of next scene's ambience 0.5-1 s before the cut is the cleanest archive-photo transition.
- VO direction: closer mic = intimacy (proximity effect, less room); speak to one person; pauses after key lines (0.5-1.5 s) let music/image answer; slower pace for memory, faster for stakes. With ElevenLabs/TTS, add pauses explicitly and vary stability.

## 6. Short-form specifics: platforms, loudness, sound-off

### Takeaway
Target ≈ −14 LUFS integrated, −1 dBTP true peak for YouTube/Reels/TikTok (observed, not published by Meta/TikTok); YouTube only turns down. TikTok is a sound-on platform (Kantar: 88% say sound is essential), but captions remain necessary for sound-off feeds.

### Cited Findings
- YouTube −14 LUFS / −1 dBTP, turns down only (observed); Instagram/Reels and TikTok publish no target, ~−14 LUFS observed, −1 dBTP advised; Facebook none published; Spotify −14 confirmed; EBU R128 −23 LUFS; ATSC A/85 −24 LKFS. "None of them publishes an official LUFS target." Hot masters get turned down and lose dynamics. Phone speakers/noisy environments favor denser signals. — [Fora Soft, 2026](https://www.forasoft.com/learn/audio-for-video/articles-audio/lufs-targets-per-platform-2026)
- TikTok/Kantar "Power of Sound" (2021): 88% of users say sound is essential to TikTok; 73% would "stop and look" at ads with audio; methodology not disclosed in article. — [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/tiktok-shares-new-insights-into-the-importance-of-sound-for-marketing-promo/601569)

### Inferences
- The current skill's "−18 LUFS" is ~4 LU quieter than the observed −14 target; since YouTube doesn't boost, the piece will play quieter than neighbors. Recommend −14 LUFS integrated, −1 dBTP, keep VO short-term loudness consistent.
- Phone speakers reproduce little below ~150-200 Hz (UNVERIFIED number): bass hits must have upper harmonics (add saturation/click layer) or they vanish; check mixes in mono and on a phone speaker; avoid wide stereo tricks that cancel in mono.
- Sound-off (Facebook/Instagram feeds) — the famous "85% muted" figure is Digiday 2016 on Facebook (not fetched; see [Digiday](https://digiday.com/media/silent-world-facebook-video/)); design so the first 2 s work silently (captions, visual hook) and sound rewards those who unmute.

## 7. Training the ear

### Takeaway
Judgment comes from reduced listening (Chion) and deliberate comparison; no fetched source gives a formal curriculum.

### Cited Findings
- Chion's reduced listening (attending to the sound's own traits rather than source/meaning) is the base exercise mode. — [Chion PDF](https://span2910.commons.gc.cuny.edu/wp-content/blogs.dir/8374/files/2019/08/Chion_Michel_Audio-Vision-edited.pdf)
- Some editors cut without the sync track to imagine the eventual sound design. — [Thom](https://filmsound.org/articles/designing_for_sound.htm)

### Inferences (exercises)
- Chion's "masking" exercise: watch a 1-min scene image-only, then sound-only, then together; note what each adds (added value).
- Swap test: put 3 contrasting tracks (empathetic, anempathetic, none) under the same 20 s cut; write the emotion each produces.
- Layer count: pause any great ad/trailer and list every simultaneous layer; check against Murch's 2.5/5 rule.
- Silence audit: mark every point a pro reel drops music; measure the lead time before the reveal.
- Daily 10-min sound walk / field recording; Twenty Thousand Hertz and Soundworks Collection for case studies.

### Gaps
- No sourced, structured ear-training program for sound designers fetched (e.g., SoundGym/critical-listening curricula not checked).
